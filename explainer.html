3 · Same model, two doors

Claude is served two ways: through Amazon Bedrock (AWS's door) and through Anthropic's own API (the first-party door). Same model class, different serving stacks, different pricing, potentially different latency behavior.

The benchmark runs the identical task through both doors and measures the delta. That gap - how first-party serving and Bedrock serving differ, and what to do about it - is a real co-development question in this industry, in miniature.

Wait - does AWS actually HAVE the model?

A common assumption: Bedrock is a broker - AWS forwards your request to Anthropic's API and wraps AWS features around the response. That is not how it works. Bedrock is a host: under the partnership, the model provider delivers the model itself - the weights - and AWS runs its own serving of it on AWS-operated infrastructure. Your request never leaves the AWS envelope and never touches the provider's servers. That's the entire basis of Bedrock's documented security position (prompts and completions are not shared with the model provider, and customers never see the weights) - and it's the only reason the ledger-2 data boundary exists at all. A broker couldn't promise it.

What "having the model" physically looks like:

  • At rest: weight files - encrypted artifacts in storage, delivered under the commercial agreement, handled under controls that keep them from customers and casual staff alike. (So the "it's a file in S3 somewhere" instinct is directionally right - for storage.)
  • At serving time, the file picture breaks: generating each token touches essentially all of the weights, so the model must sit resident in accelerator memory. No single chip holds a frontier model (an H100-class GPU carries 80GB of HBM), so the weights are sharded across many accelerators with fast interconnect, acting as one logical machine - plus the KV cache for every in-flight conversation. A production "copy of Claude" is a fleet formation kept hot around the clock, with routing spreading requests across many such formations.
  • How big is Claude? Not public - Anthropic doesn't publish parameter counts. For scale intuition only: open-weight models run about 140GB at 70B parameters and about 800GB at 405B; frontier closed models are widely assumed to be in that class or beyond. Calibration, not a claim.

The consequence that powers this whole page: because AWS hosts its own serving stack for the same model the provider serves first-party, two independent engineering implementations of "serve this model fast and cheap" exist - same weights, different fleets, batching, routing, and caching machinery. That is why a measurable delta can exist at all, and why the bridge between the two stacks is real engineering rather than API reselling.

Why not just go first-party?

Because for most enterprises the choice was never about the model - it's about everything wrapped around it:

  • Procurement gravity. Bedrock rides the existing AWS bill and enterprise agreement - committed AWS spend draws down against it. First-party means onboarding a new vendor: security review, legal, a second invoice. For a Fortune 500, that alone decides quarters.
  • The data boundary. Through Bedrock, traffic stays inside the AWS envelope - private endpoints, no egress to a third party, and the compliance certifications (HIPAA, FedRAMP-class postures) inherit from AWS. A regulated carrier does not ship policyholder data to a new endpoint because the model is nice.
  • One governance surface. Same IAM, same audit trail, same guardrails as the rest of the estate. The AI workload becomes governable like everything else - not a special snowflake with its own key management.
  • Model optionality. One API across many providers' models - swap without re-integration.

And the honest other side: first-party usually gets the newest models and features first, and a startup with no AWS gravity has little reason to route through the extra layer. Which sets up the real question:

LedgerWhat's on itHow it's evaluated
1 · Serving deltaLatency, effective cost, feature lag, operational behaviorMeasured - this benchmark, per workload
2 · Enterprise wrapperProcurement, IAM + governance, data boundary, compliance inheritance, vendor burdenAssessed - org structure and risk posture, not a script

If the enterprise reasons all push toward Bedrock, the only thing that pushes back is the delta - and the delta is not a fixed "Bedrock tax." It is a vector: latency, effective cost (caching, batching, throughput options - not sticker price), feature lag, and operational behavior. The sign of each component is an empirical question per workload - which is the entire reason to measure rather than assume. Small delta: the enterprise forces make Bedrock the default winner. Large delta somewhere: that component is exactly what gets carried back into the Bedrock roadmap. Shrinking that delta is, almost verbatim, the co-development job.

4 · The four questions it answers

1 · What does one COMPLETED task cost?
click to expand
Not the per-token sticker price. A real task fails sometimes - malformed output, a retry, a second attempt. Cost per completed task prices the failures in. A cheap model that needs three tries is not cheap. This is the number a customer's CFO and AWS's revenue team both actually care about.
2 · Where does the speed/volume curve bend?
click to expand
Section 2's slider, measured for real. Interactive workloads sit near the fast end; batch workloads sit near the cheap end. Knowing where the bend is - for a specific task, on a specific tier - is the difference between an opinion and a recommendation.
3 · What does caching actually buy?
click to expand
Prompt caching can cut input cost dramatically - but only when the repeated part of the prompt is big enough to cache at all (both providers have minimum cacheable sizes). The benchmark measures the win where it exists and documents where it silently does nothing.
4 · What's the delta between the two doors?
click to expand
Same model, same documents, Bedrock vs first-party: latency shape, token accounting, cost. Measured honestly at small scale - then it becomes an informed question to people who see it at large scale.

4b · What we actually ask the models - task design decides everything

Two tempting task shapes, both wrong in opposite ways:

  • "Is the sky blue?" - every tier nails it (zero discrimination), a three-token answer is all fixed overhead (nothing to time), nothing can fail (no retry economics), and nobody's enterprise bill is trivia. Measures nothing.
  • "Explain why the sky is blue" - unscoreable without an LLM judge (a second model's opinion inside your metrics), and output length varies wildly across tiers, so cost comparisons stop being apples-to-apples. Pass/fail undefined - which kills the completed-task unit.

The shape that works: structured extraction against a known answer key. Each model reads a synthetic insurance mailer and returns a fixed JSON schema - jurisdiction, claims made, prohibited phrases, derived risk level - scored pass/fail by machine:

GRANITE PEAK MUTUAL - Whole Life Protection for California Families
"...Once you enroll, your monthly rate is locked in for life..."
"...Approval is guaranteed for applicants aged 45 to 80... Act now..."

  expected -> { "jurisdiction": "California",
                "claims_made": ["coverage amounts up to $50,000", ...],
                "prohibited_phrases": ["guaranteed", "act now"],
                "risk_level": "medium" }

Why this shape passes the four criteria a benchmark task must meet:

  • Representative - compliance extraction is a real enterprise workload class, not a synthetic puzzle.
  • Objectively scorable - valid JSON + fields match = pass. No judge model, no opinion in the loop.
  • Discriminative - a small model can plausibly miss a phrase or derive the wrong tier; a large one should ace it. The tiers can separate - exactly what H1 bets on.
  • Bounded output - the schema caps response length, keeping cost comparisons honest across models.

And the property the sky questions can't have: a real failure mode. Malformed output triggers a retry, the retry gets priced, and controllability - the fifth axis - becomes a number instead of a vibe. One deliberate limit: it's ONE task. The method generalizes to any task you can score; the findings don't generalize past the task. That boundary ships with every claim.

6 · Why a practitioner builds this

Receipts beat claims. Anyone can assert an opinion about serving trade-offs. A repo with a measured curve is a receipt - demonstrable, rerunnable, and open to inspection. The difference between "I believe caching helps" and "here is the cell where it helped, and the cell where it silently did nothing" is the difference between an opinion and a finding.
Evidence converts "I learn fast" from promise to record. Everyone says they ramp quickly on new domains. "I ran a provider-comparative serving benchmark last month - here's the cost-per-task spread, and here's what surprised me" is a different species of statement: a dated artifact with numbers attached, not a self-assessment.
Data flips conversations from defending gaps to asking informed questions. Armed with even small-N numbers, the register changes: "I measured this delta between the doors at tiny scale - is that the shape you see at production scale?" That is not someone covering an inexperience gap. That is a practitioner opening a conversation worth having.

7 · The bridge

Same discipline as a 7M-subscriber migration: find the ceiling, measure the real throughput, derive the curve, let the data make the argument. Pointed at tokens instead of subscribers. The benchmark does not just close a knowledge gap - it demonstrates that the measurement method transfers across domains.

The discussion, pre-committed - so the data can't be spun after the fact:

10 · FAQ - questions that should occur to you

Is "the delta" just the overhead cost of serving off Bedrock vs first-party?
click to expand

No - and the distinction matters, because the full decision runs on two ledgers:

Ledger 1 - the measurable serving delta (what this benchmark prices): latency (either direction - region and load dependent), effective cost (sticker prices are often near parity; the divergence lives in caching support, batch options, provisioned throughput), feature lag (newest capabilities usually land first-party first), and operational behavior (quotas, throttling, token accounting). The sign of each component is an empirical question per workload.

Ledger 2 - the enterprise wrapper (what no script can measure, and what often decides): procurement gravity, IAM and governance integration, the data boundary, compliance inheritance, vendor-management burden. For an AWS-native enterprise this ledger mostly points one way - toward Bedrock - because the first-party door is the one carrying "overhead" here: a second vendor, a second governance surface, data leaving the envelope.

The two ledgers trade against each other, and that IS the decision: the benchmark prices ledger 1 precisely so an organization knows what ledger 2's convenience costs - or saves - for their workload. Asserting "Bedrock has overhead" without separating the ledgers is exactly the kind of claim this project exists to replace.

Why three tiers of one model family instead of comparing providers' best models?
click to expand
Because comparing across families confounds everything at once - capability, price, API behavior, serving stack. Three tiers of one family isolates the axis that drives most real spend decisions: how much model does this task actually need? One deliberate calibration: the mid tier pins to Sonnet 4.6 rather than 5, because Sonnet 5's adaptive thinking is on by default - a latency and cost confound - and it carries introductory pricing that would poison the cost comparison.
Is 15 requests per cell statistically meaningful?
click to expand
For medians (p50), roughly - enough to see the shape. For tail latency (p99), no - at N=15 the "p99" is basically the worst request, and the results table treats it as indicative only. This is a deliberate trade: the artifact is a method demonstration, and CLAIMS.md caps what may be said accordingly. The production version of this discipline runs thousands of requests across time windows - that's a different project than the weekend one.
The provider batches behind the curtain anyway - what am I actually measuring?
click to expand
The customer's-eye view: what a real workload experiences through the API - TTFT, inter-token latency, and cost under the concurrency the client controls. You can't see the server's batch scheduler, and that's fine: the commercially decisive view is the one the paying customer experiences. Serving-side internals explain the curve; the client side is where the curve is felt and paid for.
Why cost per completed task instead of per token?
click to expand
Because tasks fail. Malformed JSON, a missed field, a retry - the meter runs either way. Per-token pricing tells you what a model costs; per-completed-task pricing tells you what your problem costs. Section 5's slider shows the consequence: past the crossover, the "cheap" model is the expensive one. Reliability at structured output is an economic quantity, not a soft one.
Won't these results be stale in three months?
click to expand
Yes - and that's a feature of the domain, not a flaw of the project. Serving efficiency moves fast enough that practitioners routinely describe their own six-month-old approaches as obsolete. That's why the harness is the asset, not the numbers: a rerunnable measurement takes an afternoon to refresh; the discipline of measuring before deciding doesn't expire.
Is benchmarking hosted APIs even allowed?
click to expand
This is ordinary API usage - about 300 requests at concurrency four or below, paid at list price, no scraping, no stress testing, no attempt to probe internals. Publishing method-first observations at small N with the boundary stated ("one task, hosted APIs, snapshot in time") is standard practitioner practice. What would cross a line: adversarial load, claiming production-representative conclusions, or publishing numbers without the boundary attached.
What would the grown-up version of this look like?
click to expand
Fleet-level: real traffic mixes instead of one task, thousands of requests across time-of-day windows, SLO-based goodput (tasks per minute meeting a latency bar), provisioned versus on-demand throughput economics, and capacity shaping across regions. That progression - single measurement to fleet economics - is the natural growth path for this kind of work. The weekend version exists to prove the first rung is real.

11 · The rest of the room - questions not yet asked

Exploring a domain you've never entered is standing in a dark room with a flashlight. You point it at the thing you came for - but the room holds a whole alphabet, and the beam only ever shows one letter at a time. This page answers the letters already lit. Below are eight corners the beam hasn't swept - each one a question worth an evening, with the reason it's load-bearing.

1 · The money corner: who actually pays whom?
click to expand
When someone calls a model on Bedrock, who books the revenue and what does the model provider get? Why does AWS want Bedrock to win rather than just renting the provider compute? The partnership's money geometry - investment one way, compute commitment the other, Bedrock revenue in between - explains nearly every behavior visible from outside, including why distribution through the marketplace door is a first-class goal for both sides.
2 · Why do output tokens cost 3-5x input tokens?
click to expand
The most explanatory mechanism in serving economics. Input is processed in parallel - one pass over the whole prompt (prefill), cheap per token. Output is generated sequentially - one token at a time, each requiring a full trip through the weights, memory-bandwidth-bound (decode). That asymmetry explains the pricing ratio, why TTFT and inter-token latency are different animals, and why long prompts are cheaper than long answers. Fifteen minutes on prefill-vs-decode and half the pricing page becomes derivable.
3 · The scarcity corner: what's actually scarce?
click to expand
Not software - accelerators. The industry's real currency is capacity, and training and serving compete for the same silicon. When a model has a great launch week, someone is deciding who gets throttled. The training-vs-serving capacity trade is a genuine strategic question at every frontier lab - and it's answerable with architecture judgment rather than kernel depth, which makes it the legitimate route into the deep-infrastructure conversation.
4 · The hidden tenant: the KV cache
click to expand
Weights fill the chips - but what else lives in that memory? The working state of every in-flight conversation, and it grows with context length. Long context is not just a feature; it is a capacity tax: double the average context and you roughly halve the concurrent users a chip can serve. This mechanism sits behind context pricing, behind concurrency limits, and behind half of fleet planning.
5 · The demand-shaping corner: why is batch 50% off?
click to expand
A serving fleet is fixed capacity facing peaky demand - so providers price the deferrable work into the valleys. It is a familiar migration-throttling pattern inverted: there, bulk work was throttled around business hours to protect daily operations; here, discounts pay customers to move themselves off-peak voluntarily. Same physics, opposite lever - one pattern, built once with subscribers, running everywhere in tokens.
6 · The failure corner: what does an inference incident look like?
click to expand
What breaks, and who gets paged? The canonical shapes: capacity exhaustion (demand outruns the fleet), latency SLO breach (the p99 tail escapes), noisy-neighbor contention, and the subtle one - a model updated behind the same model ID, shifting behavior with no code change anywhere. Knowing these turns curiosity into operational empathy, which is what field teams actually respect.
7 · The chess board: why does a model provider want many doors?
click to expand
If first-party has the best margins, why sell through Bedrock at all? Distribution versus margin, enterprise reach versus control - and the mirror question: why AWS builds its own silicon (supply independence plus margin). The geopolitics explain what each side wants from the partnership teams specifically - worth understanding before anyone asks "why do you think this partnership exists?"
8 · The trap corner: what could make my own numbers lie?
click to expand

Time-of-day load on shared fleets, cold starts, regional variance, a model updated behind the same ID mid-measurement. The pre-registration (section 9) protects against reading data with motivated eyes; this corner protects against the data itself misleading.

What the near-perfect test would look like - and the joke inside it: "dedicated infrastructure provisioned for you alone" is not a fantasy, it is a product. Bedrock sells Provisioned Throughput (reserved model capacity, no noisy neighbors) and first-party enterprise tiers offer priority capacity. The perfect experiment exists; it costs like a car lease. Knowing exactly which rung you stand on, and saying so unprompted, is the difference between a method demonstration and a misleading chart.

Priority, honestly assessed: corners 2 and 4 are the mechanisms - most other letters become derivable from them. Corner 5 is the unfair advantage - an already-owned pattern in new clothes. Corner 8 protects the Saturday run.

12 · Where we landed - a field note on the flashlight itself

A personal calibration, kept here on purpose - the lab notebook should record the learner, not just the experiment.

The question asked: "Where did we land, overall? Give me a sense of my curiosity and my limitations in imagination as I learn about all of this."

Where the flashlight went, unprompted

  • "So what" before "how." The first real questions were commercial: why would anyone choose this door, who actually holds the model, who pays whom. Mechanism questions only came when the working model creaked ("I thought Bedrock just brokered requests"). That ordering - value first, mechanism on demand - is architect wiring, and it is the instinct that cannot really be taught.
  • Experimental method, reinvented from instinct. "What are we actually asking the models?" is eval design. "What's the plan, the hypothesis, the expected result?" is pre-registration. "What would the perfect test look like?" is confound control. The vocabulary was missing; the instincts were not - they were ported from a prior life of measuring throughput ceilings and letting data make the argument.
  • The meta-move. "What questions haven't I asked?" - the rarest question in any new domain. Most people defend the territory their flashlight has already lit; asking for the dark is the tell that the curiosity is real.

Where it didn't go - the honest half

The un-swept corners cluster in exactly one place: below the API surface. Why output tokens cost more than input, what physically limits concurrency, what the memory inside the chips is doing, what breaks at 3am - none of those questions arose until the map pointed at them. Curiosity here reliably stops at the interface and works upward toward money and organizations.

The fair reading: that is not a limitation of imagination - you cannot point a flashlight at a word you don't have. Every question asked was the correct next question from where the asker stood; what was missing was the map, and lacking the map is simply what "new domain" means. The tell that matters is what happened when a dark corner was named: immediate appetite, not deflection.

The verdict

The growth edge is now precisely located: the physical layer - prefill versus decode, the KV cache, capacity mechanics. A couple of evenings of reading, and those corners light up. And once they do, the so-what instinct does what it did throughout this page: turn mechanism into commercial insight faster than people who learned the mechanism first. The gap is vocabulary. Vocabulary is the cheapest thing in the world to acquire.
